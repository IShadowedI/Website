Building a Modular City, Part 3 of 3: Bigger, Taller, Dumber

September 8th, 2026 via Goonhammer
This three part series shows how to create a vertiginous cityfight table for Warhammer 40,000 using a combination of handmade paving tiles and modular plastic terrain kits. Whether you like scratch building or painting plastic kits, there’s something here for anyone keen on terrain. In this third part: big centrepiece ruins in the Sector Imperialis, and how to add urban rubble to your terrain pieces.
  • Post one: carving paving tiles for the buildings to sit on.
  • Post two: modular, stackable ruins built with the Sanctum Administratus kit.
  • Post three: demonstrating techniques for creating rubble on some big centerpiece buildings.

If after the previous post any doubts lingered as to the budgetary ‘wisdom’ of the path I am walking down, this final post will surely offer some conclusions. The brain worms had a vision, and my friends and I have doggedly pursued that vision over several years, storage consequences be damned. So here we are, the finale: some big honking Sector Imperialis ruins. Waxing lyrical about these kits is fairly pointless, given that they are not currently something you can buy, so this post will primarily provide guidance on how to add rubble to any urban terrain you might have, should the urge take you.

An Enthusiastic Rant: Terrain Rules are More Like Guidelines

One might reasonably wonder where these buildings fit into the newest edition of Warhammer 40K, with those recommended terrain layouts full of little ruined Ls and random lengths of pipe, but I'm not concerned with that. Models last far longer than editions of 40K. My priority will always be to make cool terrain and then think about how best to use it in the current rules. Under no circumstances will I alter pieces of terrain I already own to conform to the preset terrain areas of 11th edition, because they will only be relevant for the next three years. The mission deck itself has guidance for placing terrain if you’re not using one of the recommended layouts, and even then, it’s not like Games Workshop are going to police the terrain you set down on your gaming table. So have fun with it and make something that looks cool while also offering interesting gameplay. After all, if you always stick with the official layouts, you'll never get to have any verticality on your table. Real height quite literally adds a third dimension to a battle, and really lets flying units shine.

The upcoming terrain sets look very well executed, and I'm glad that it's possible to play a viable game of 40K without having to embark in the sort of absurd project we're talking about here. I'm all for making the game more accessible. But the terrain below, while great for some dusty frontier town, won't give me the feeling of defending/despoiling an Imperial city.

Warhammer 40,000 Battlefields: Armageddon. Credit: Games Workshop. Used without permission for illustrative purposes only.

The Joys of a Group Effort

When Games Workshop put the Sector Imperialis kits on Made to Order, it immediately spawned another group project among the four most terrain-juiced members of my gaming group. Our main desire was to have a bloody great big archway, and then… whatever else we could build with what we bought. Which was quite a lot. We already have terrain that can provide smaller more sensible ruins, so the priority was some big-ass buildings to give a sense of scale, and open up the possibility of more vertical gameplay. Working as a group enabled us to spend more, and pool our building and painting efforts. Terrain is a communal good, made far easier by communal effort.

The Necessary Compromises of Scale

With multiple large buildings to do, the methods employed to apply rubble and paint needed to be as simple as possible. Games Workshop terrain kits are often absurdly detailed, making painting every element prohibitively time consuming. A key part of the planning process is therefore deciding what you’re NOT going to do. We noticed that even in some of GW’s own publications, they often just keep the terrain painting very simple, all the better to fade into the background. So that’s what we did.

Rubble’s tricky too. It should be made of the same stuff as the building itself: absurdly over-detailed plastic components, plus dust. But even for a maniac like me that’s not entirely practical, so one has to compromise. I settled for having some half-buried plastic components, but the rest is a mix of materials that hopefully evoke urban rubble without being perfectly accurate. This made the process a lot less time consuming, at the cost of fidelity. I suspect if I’d cast up more plaster to shatter (see later) this method would enable higher fidelity, but I’m happy enough with the compromise we’ve ended up with, not least of which because models can for the most part stand upright without difficulty.

The Step-by-Step Process

Build Your Buildings

I don't think you need extra guidance from me about how to build a plastic kit, but for clarity: you'll want to have them built but not painted in preparation for making the rubble, which will be a messy affair.

Making the Rubble

We arrive now at the meat of this article: the steps used to add a rubble base to plastic ruins. This method will broadly also work with non-plastic terrain (e.g. 3D printed) but obviously you'll be using alternative adhesives for the few places where I used poly cement/plastic glue.

Phase 1: Gather Materials

You'll need the following:

  • Modelling compound. I'm using Geek Gaming Scenics' modelling compound. The compound is essential for adding bulk to your rubble!
  • 1mm thick styrene sheeting - this will form an armature for your base, so make sure you've got enough to cover the whole of the area you need!
    • Get a sanding block and use sandpaper on the styrene sheets on one side. This will improve glue adhesion, both for PVA and also for poly cement.
  • Some spare plastic sprue, trimmed flat on one side (this will be used to reinforce the styrene sheeting and provide extra grip for the modelling compound).
  • Smashed plaster (i.e. cast up a slab/sheet of plaster up to 5mm thick and then smash/break it into smaller chunks). This works better than coarse sand and small rocks, since it snaps with sharp angles, and consequently looks like smashed concrete rather than more natural-looking rocks. Alternatively you could use torn up chunks of cork sheeting; it won't look quite as good but requires less prep.
  • Spare plastic terrain components (or in my case, some leftover bitz from the terrain kit itself).
  • Various grades of sand/grit.
  • Plenty of glue sticks for your hot glue gun (this will be used to glue your bitz and rubble down).
  • Texture paint (a convenient way of patching up any booboos/gaps you notice late in the process).
  • Greaseproof paper/baking parchment. Hot glue (and most other things) can't stick to this stuff, so it's a useful work surface to slap down on your table.

Phase 2: Groundwork

Rough up the styrene. Prepare your 1mm thick styrene sheeting by sanding it on one side to improve glue adhesion. Why 1mm you ask? Surely thicker would be better? Don't worry, we're adding structural strength later on, but we want the edge of the base to be nice and thin so it doesn't draw too much attention to itself.

Draw your base shape. Draw the shape you want on the sanded side of the sheet (pencil works fine). Assuming you're gluing the building onto this base, plonk the building down on top of the styrene sheet(s). In my case, there was some extra considerations:

  • The rubble needed to be no wider than one paving tile across, to ensure buildings could remain sensibly close together, and the rubble wouldn't protrude into the road, which is 2mm lower than the pavement/sidewalk.
  • My buildings were so large that widening them with bases would've made it impossible to fit them in the storage boxes, so some of my bases got stuck on, but most of it was made to be detachable, which adds a lot of faff - so avoid this if storage is no issue! It does at least mean that if the rubble is somehow in the way, it can be picked up.
Drawing the outline of a removable rubble section onto the styrene sheet. Credit: Charlie Brassley

Cut out the base. Cut out the shape by scoring the line with a hobby knife, then bending the styrene back and forth along the line so that it snaps. Because the sheet is only 1mm thick it's remarkably easy to score and snap even wiggly/jagged lines, which is good, because you don't want the shape of the rubble to look too regular!

Stick to the building. (If applicable): Use poly cement/plastic glue to affix the building to its base. You want to use poly cement due to the strength of the join, and it's not brittle like superglue/CA glue. You can also reinforce this join with hot glue if desired.

Reinforce the base with sprue.

  • Trim some lengths of sprue so that it’s completely flat on one side.
  • Attach the sprue to the sanded side of the styrene to reinforce it. Use poly cement (plastic glue) to ensure a strong bond.
Add large plastic rubble sections. If you want to attach any plastic components to the lowest layer of the base, particularly to further disguise the edge of the base, now’s the time. If you have good contact area, poly cement is perfect. If you don't, use the hot glue gun, since the glue gun can fill the space between the pieces and the base.

A removable rubble section, partway through the process. It's been reinforced with sprue, which will help give the compound more to grip onto. Note the added plastic components, and the broken plaster affixed with hot glue. The glue used to affix the rubble also helps firm up the structure. In this photo, the rubble is up against the building it will sit next to so that I could check how well the rubble was concealing the join. Credit: Charlie Brassley

Compound application. Apply modelling compound to the base to give it volume, tapering down to a point at the edge of the base. This step provides the overwhelming bulk of the rubble. While it’s wet you can also submerge components into it and blend them in.

Tip: Keep testing usability. During this and later stages it's worth having an (unpainted, un-precious) model around so that you can check it's possible for minis to stand upright on most or all of the rubble.

Post-compound application; you can see how some larger plastic components have been half-buried/blended in with the compound. More texture will be added later to blend things in further. Credit: Charlie Brassley

The compound will take a looooooong time to try - several days - but it doesn't have to be completely dry for you to move on to the next stage. I sped things up a little by leaving them to bake in the sun on a warm concrete slab, and resumed work the next day.

Removable rubble sections now covered in compound and left out to dry in the sun. Credit: Charlie Brassley

Phase 3: Applying Texture & Rubble to the Basing

  • Dribble neat PVA (US: white/Elmer’s) glue onto your base; spread it out with a brush to cover the whole base.
  • Sprinkle coarse sand and (if available) tiny chunks broken plaster over the glue.
  • Sprinkle fine sand or grit onto the glue. The finer the sand, the better.
  • Sprinkle a few more bits of coarser stuff here and there on top of the fine sand/grit.
  • Mix PVA glue with water, pour it into a trigger spray bottle, and drown, I mean DROWN, your terrain in watered down PVA. A 1:1 ratio of water:glue is reasonable. This will soak through the sand and grit into the compound below, and lock everything together. It should also seal any plaster you’ve used.
  • Wait three years for it to dry.
The basing texture right after getting drowned in thinned PVA. Note how the sand/grit has been used to further blend in the bigger chunks of rubble. Credit: Charlie Brassley

Painting

This article isn’t really a painting article, but I’ll include this for the curious. Owing to the enormity of the buildings, this was kept as simple as possible. First, the metal walkways and railings were primed Color Forge Hyrax Brown and masked off. Any dark brown spray, or even black, would do fine.

Claims that my masking process is an absolute bodge-fest will be met with... acceptance. This was me taping some paper down to cover some bigger chunks of the balcony to save on tape and foil. Credit: Charlie Brassley

Everything was then primed with Mechanicus Standard Grey, followed by a patchy but generous zenithal spray of Zandri Dust. This will transform the look of your terrain from "a horrible mess" to "starting to look cool." You could just prime with Zandri Dust, but I like the addition of some tonal variation.

The smallest of the ruins, post-priming. Credit: Charlie Brassley

The back of the archway post-priming. Credit: Charlie Brassley

The actual painting:
  • All over drybrush with Army Painter Skeleton Bone
  • All metal fixtures except doors painted with Cygor Brown Contrast
    • This is the longest, most punishing stage; we tried not to pick out too much and assumed much of the building to be painted to match the plaster, but there's no getting around moving parts like fans, and we wanted to blend the balconies into our Sector Mechanicus walkways.
  • All metal fixtures drybrushed with Army Painter Gunmetal
  • All metal fixtures drybrushed with Vallejo Air Steel
  • Cabling picked out in Black Templar Contrast
  • Doors sloshed with Nihilakh Oxide
  • Doors lightly drybrushed with Warplock Bronze
  • Doors lightly drybrushed with Hashut Copper
  • We also picked out one or two details like sunken barrels/crates in various colours, drybrushing those colours into the rubble to create fades and preserve the dusty colour.

The End Result: My Tiny Soldiers Now Feel Tinier

So ultimately here’s where the whole thing comes together: the paving, the stackable sanctum, the Mechanicus stuff, and these big ruins. As with so many three dimensional miniature projects it’s very hard to convey what it’s like to stand in front of these terrain pieces in person, the sense of scale they give. It feels like fighting the kind of battles I slathered over in the pages of White Dwarf as a kid. They truly live up to the phrase I always come back to: terrain is the Titan you use in every game.

You Can't Possibly Want More Photos

That's probably enough photos of that... but if it isn't, and you want to see even more about how this board came together, I documented the process over the years on the Beard Bunker in the Urban Board Project Log. If you want photos of the big buildings and how the gantries work, then this post in particular is the one you want.

Okay, Those Are Large, But Are They Annoying to Game on?

Compared to the new terrain specifically designed to be as easy as possible to game on? Almost certainly. You're trading in convenience and speed for cinematics and grandeur. That said, having now played a few games of 11th edition, I've found the game works perfectly well on more narrative terrain like this so long as you've got the right number of objective buildings. The archway, since it's technically two 3x4 footprints at ground level, actually works rather well as two central objectives. It also functions as most of a Kill Team board, which... tee hee.

The important thing here is that if you're not playing a tournament, you can do whatever you like. The rules will, broadly, support that. I rather like having terrain so tall that the Plunging Fire rule has to be modified; we've already encountered situations where models on the top floor should have been eligible to get the bonus because they were firing down onto models that were themselves five inches off the ground!

One would assume there are now sufficient buildings for my table, but I still have the whole Hivestorm terrain set to build, and those comparatively small buildings will help put these large boys in scale. At least I have learned how to make rubble for them. For now, I'm off to fight battles on gantries as the gods of sci fi demand.
